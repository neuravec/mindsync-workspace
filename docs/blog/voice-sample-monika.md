## #17: Who decided adults don't get to play?

Somewhere between your first job and now, playtime has probably started to slowly disappear from your life. What tends to happen is the fact that the things we do for pleasure get taken out by things that feel more important.

To see friends, you begin to schedule meetings a month in advance, your chores become your daily routine, and your daily energy capacity needs to be well thought out, just in case you do one thing too much and get burned out. Ultimately, "what do you do for fun" becomes a question you have to think about before answering. The stereotypical response is usually some version of "I watch things in the evening" or "I don't really have time for that anymore".

We tend to treat play as something you grow out of, a phase like loose teeth and afternoon naps. The approach of being a fully functioning and self-sufficient adult replaces it, and any leftover playfulness gets rebranded as a hobby you do now and again. Ideally, it's required to be something productive, monetisable, or at least improving you in some measurable way. Would you agree that life shouldn't look like this?

**Play isn't a thing you grow out of:**

Researchers studying adult playfulness have found that adults who engage in playful activities cope better with stress, experience more positive emotions, show greater resilience when facing challenges, and report higher life satisfaction. This benefit doesn't expire once you turn the ripe age of 18.

A 2022 study of nearly 700 adults during the pandemic found that playfulness directly shaped how people coped with extreme stress. The playful ones leaned toward adaptive strategies, like reframing a situation or accepting what they couldn't change, and felt more able to handle what was in front of them. Playfulness worked as a genuine psychological resource, the same way resilience and optimism do.

The mechanism is straightforward: play triggers the release of endorphins, lowers cortisol, and shifts the nervous system out of the low-grade threat state a lot of us live in by default. It does, biochemically, what most of us are chasing with apps, supplements, and carefully optimised evening routines.

**How do adults play?**

When you hear fun, do you picture board games or sports? Some sort of structured activity? Creative workshops? Festivals?

I feel like in adulthood, play is less about specific toys or games and more about how you approach everyday experiences. The same walk to the shop can be a chore or a small adventure, depending entirely on how you approach it. It's about looking at an ordinary moment and letting it become something you enjoy, rather than just one more thing to get through.

- Brazilian jiu jitsu, bouldering, dancing, cycling, or some faintly intimidating physical activity you take up in your thirties (important: be prepared that it'll probably take over your entire life)
- Caring for indoor plants and developing genuine feelings about a single orchid
- Free or low-cost local events you find by literally googling "free events near me"
- Volunteering somewhere you actually care about, which turns out to feel better than most things you'd pay for
- Birdwatching, mapmaking your own region, imagine what you would do if you were unemployed and had all day to yourself

Disclaimer: don't turn fun into a to-do list!

I'll be honest, I'm not good at this one. I have a documented habit of turning every interest into a project with goals and metrics, which is roughly the fastest way to drain the joy out of anything. Learning to do something purely because it's fun, with no secondary purpose, has been harder than I'd like to admit.

I'd genuinely love to know what fun looks like for you these days. What do you do?

## #24: Asking again won't change the answer (but you knew that)

**Anxious attachment: the one who never knows if the stove is still on**

Do any of these sound familiar?

- Someone takes longer than usual to reply, and before you know it there's a whole theory forming as to why that is.
- Reassurance never lasts. You've asked "are we okay?", believed the answer, and still found yourself asking again a few days later. Just to be sure.
- You locked the front door. You know you did. Are you sure though?

Anxious attachment forms early, when a child's nervous system learns that a bond is something you fight for daily, or else it disappears. Comfort came unpredictably. Some days it was there, some days it wasn't, and there was no way to tell which day it would be. Crying louder worked just often enough to never stop doing it. A behaviour that pays off even once in a while becomes the one you reach for first. Years later, adulthood swaps out everything except the strategy.

Fun fact: slot machines pay out on what psychologists call a variable ratio schedule. The win might come after three pulls or three hundred, and it's exactly that unpredictability that makes stopping feel impossible.

**As an adult, it tends to look like this:**

You notice every change in tone. A shorter reply, a full stop where there wasn't one. Everyone else seems not to take it as seriously while you're already working out what you did wrong.

Closeness often comes with a countdown. What you want is connection; what you feel inside it is tension intertwined with fear and a readiness for it to end. Waiting for abandonment is exhausting work, and it has never once prevented one.

Surprisingly, research has something kind to say here. In 2006, Fraley and colleagues showed people films of faces slowly changing expression. Those high in attachment anxiety spotted the change earlier than everyone else. The radar is real. They were also less accurate because they called it too soon, and when everyone had to watch the full film, the accuracy gap disappeared. Your perception works fine. The timing is what trips it up.

**The work comes down to three things, in this order:**

- Settle the body before you do anything else. An anxious spiral is an internal alarm going off, and nobody makes good decisions in the middle of a panic. Breathe, take a walk, drink some water. Then decide what to do, once the decision is yours and the emotions are no longer making it for you.
- Recognize the moment when your trigger takes over. Your panic mode has specific triggers, and they differ from person to person. For example, it can vary from a reply that took a day, the "sure" where there used to be a paragraph, to the plans made without you. Once you know yours, "something is wrong with us" turns into "ah, it's that trigger again."
- Say what you need in the simplest form. "I'm feeling unsure about us and could use some warmth today" does more good than three days of testing them and watching for signs. The desperate fight for the bond is the one strategy guaranteed to strain it.

One thing for the people in a relationship with someone anxious. You are not their therapist, and wounds you didn't make aren't yours to carry. But you can be something the research calls a corrective experience:

- Every hard conversation that ends with nobody leaving is evidence.
- Every reply that arrives without a catastrophe attached is evidence.
- One piece alone changes nothing. Years of them can rewrite the pattern.

So if you love an anxious person, you don't need to fix anything. Be predictable. Say what you mean, do what you said, and let time prove the rest.

This is what MindSync's Between-Session Notes are for. Log the message you reread, the moment the alarm went off, and what you did next. Give it time, and Symptom Tracking turns those entries into something you can see: certain people, certain moments, the same switch every time. Then flag what matters into your Workspace and bring it to your next session, so you arrive at therapy with something solid to work with.

When someone goes quiet, what's the narrative your brain creates?
